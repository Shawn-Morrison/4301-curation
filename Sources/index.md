# Initial Source Annotations

These six sources are organized by the collection area they inform. They are the starting evidence base for the curation; they do not guarantee that an app is safe for every person or situation.

## All skill levels > Confidence and safety

### [Older Adults and Technology: Moving Beyond the Stereotypes](https://longevity.stanford.edu/older-adults-and-technology-moving-beyond-the-stereotypes/)

**Category placement:** All skill levels > Confidence and safety  
**Annotation:** This Stanford Center on Longevity article examines older adults’ technology use, digital-literacy gaps, misinformation, and the effect of stereotypes on confidence. It is valuable because it supports the curation’s central idea that older adults are capable of learning technology when instruction is purposeful, patient, and confidence-building. It is most useful for caregivers, educators, and family members who need context for why the collection starts with manageable tasks rather than assumptions about age. Its focus is broad digital literacy rather than app instructions, and it was published in 2019, so it should guide the approach rather than serve as current app-specific advice.

### [Technology for Seniors Made Easy](https://treasureyourlifenow.com/)

**Category placement:** All skill levels > Confidence and safety  
**Annotation:** Sheila Finkelstein’s Technology for Seniors resource describes a teaching approach centered on helping "boomers" and seniors feel secure using technology and social media to connect with others. It is valuable because it reinforces the project’s emphasis on connection and confidence rather than treating older users as incapable. It is most useful for an older adult or family member looking for approachable, video-based technology learning. The resource is creator-led rather than a peer-reviewed study, and the page points to a channel and related materials instead of one narrowly defined tutorial. It should therefore supplement, not replace, current official app help.

## Intermediate > Social

### [Best Social Media Apps for Seniors in 2026](https://www.seniorliving.org/cell-phone/apps/social-media/)

**Category placement:** Intermediate > Social  
**Annotation:** This SeniorLiving.org guide compares social media options that older adults may use to connect with family, friends, hobbies, and local communities. It is valuable because it discusses Facebook, YouTube, and other services in an older-adult context and includes practical limitations, such as contentious posts on neighborhood platforms. It is most useful for an older adult or helper deciding whether a social platform matches a specific interest. The page is current and has expert review, but SeniorLiving.org is a commercial publisher that may use affiliate or commission relationships. It also surveys many apps rather than providing step-by-step safety instruction for one person.

### [Older Adults’ Use of Facebook Continues to Grow](https://www.aarp.org/home-family/personal-technology/info-2018/facebook-older-americans-privacy.html)

**Category placement:** Intermediate > Social > Facebook  
**Annotation:** This AARP article reports on Facebook adoption among adults 50 and older and highlights their concerns about the use of personal data. It is valuable because it supports Facebook’s placement as a useful but more cautious social option: it can be familiar and widely used, yet privacy cannot be ignored. It is most useful for a reader deciding whether Facebook belongs beyond the beginner level and for a helper planning a privacy conversation before setup. The article is from 2018 and its polling figures are not current platform statistics. It provides context, not a current privacy-settings tutorial or a guarantee of safety.

### [News Use Across Social Media Platforms 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2016/05/26/news-use-across-social-media-platforms-2016/)

**Category placement:** Intermediate > Social > Facebook and YouTube  
**Annotation:** This Pew Research Center report analyzes how U.S. adults encountered news across major social media services, including Facebook and YouTube. It is valuable because it explains why information quality belongs in a safety-focused collection: people may encounter news while using a platform for another purpose. It is most useful for readers and helpers who need a research-based reason to discuss pausing, checking sources, and avoiding automatic trust in social content. The study is methodologically transparent, but it is from 2016 and measures news use rather than older-adult app skill. It is background evidence, not a recommendation to use a platform.

## Intermediate > Entertainment

### [Choosing a Kindle Fire for the Senior in Your Life](https://www.assistedliving.org/best-tablets-for-seniors/choosing-a-kindle-fire-for-the-senior-in-your-life/)

**Category placement:** Intermediate > Entertainment > Device context  
**Annotation:** This AssistedLiving.org article reviews Kindle Fire devices as reading and entertainment tools and notes features such as larger text, media access, and app downloads. It is valuable because it adds device context to the collection: whether an app feels approachable can depend on screen size, text readability, and how easily a person can reach familiar entertainment. It is most useful for a family member considering a tablet for reading, videos, or simple apps. The article is centered on a specific device line and is older, so its model details and prices should not be treated as current. It does not evaluate every app’s safety or usability.
